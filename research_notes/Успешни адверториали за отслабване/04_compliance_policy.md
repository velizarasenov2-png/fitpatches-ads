# Compliance map: weight-loss advertorials for a berberine transdermal patch (Bulgaria/EU, Meta traffic)

Scope note: research as of 3 Oct 2026. These notes separate what the law or policy text says from how practitioners read it. This is not legal advice. "Verified" means I read the primary text in this session. "Secondary" means a law-firm, trade-press or practitioner source. Anything I could not verify is listed under Gaps.

## 1. Meta Advertising Standards (2025–2026): weight loss, personal attributes, deception, landing pages, health-data restrictions, advertorial enforcement

### Takeaway
Meta rewrote its Health & Wellness ad policy on **22 Jul 2026**. It now reviews ads claim by claim. Weight-loss products must still target 18+. It bans: statements of inferiority about appearance, "pinching fat" close-ups, cure claims for a listed set of diseases that includes **Diabetes and Thyroid**, sensational or clickbait health language, and, most relevant to a patch, **"claims that results can be achieved solely by using wearable products"**. Separately, since January 2025 Meta has restricted lower-funnel event sharing (Purchase, AddToCart and so on) for domains it classifies as Health & Wellness. Supplement names and weight-loss claims are known classification triggers. For a weight-loss patch domain, the biggest day-to-day risks are ad rejection, account restriction and domain-level data restriction. Legal action is less likely day to day.

### Cited Findings
**Health & Wellness policy (primary, verified; page shows "Last Updated: Jul 22, 2026")** — [Meta Health & Wellness](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness/)
- 18+ targeting is required for ads for weight loss/gain products, cosmetic procedures/surgeries and reproductive health products. The policy also says it restricts dietary-supplement ads from targeting under-18s. — [Meta H&W](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness/)
- Prohibited in weight-loss/gain and cosmetic ads: ads that "Contain[s] statements of inferiority about physical appearance (i.e., terms, descriptions, or questions that are negative and attack an individual's appearance, specific body parts, or hygiene)"; a "close up on specific body area by pinching fat"; and "Claims that results can be achieved solely by using wearable products". — [Meta H&W](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness/)
- Also prohibited: ads that "Employ clickbait tactics in a health, weight loss, or weight gain context, such as sensational language with exaggerated or extreme claims, or promises of specific outcomes within a set timeframe without disclaimers or qualifiers." — [Meta H&W](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness/)
- Also prohibited: "Claims (including those from health professionals or health organizations) to cure, heal, or eliminate any of the following incurable diseases and/or terminal illnesses". The list includes Diabetes, Herpes, Thyroid, Psoriasis, Ebola, Cancer, Autism, Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, ALS and HIV. — [Meta H&W](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness/)
- Allowed for 18+ (supplements): "The ad can illustrate people using the product or the service, and its impact of using it, and clearly indicate the time taken to achieve noticeable results." Also allowed: "General cosmetic products, procedures, surgeries depicting before and after transformation." — [Meta H&W](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness/)
- **Conflicting secondary readings on weight-loss before/after:** AdLigator (on the 22 Jul 2026 update) says before/after is "allowed if paired with compliant claims", and that 18+ is required for supplements only when the copy contains a weight-loss or weight-gain claim — [AdLigator](https://adligator.com/blog/meta-health-wellness-ad-policy-update-2026). Accelerated Digital Media (27 Aug 2026) says the before/after allowance covers cosmetic products only and "does NOT extend to weight-loss products specifically" — [ADM](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/). The primary text names only "general cosmetic products, procedures, surgeries" for before/after.

**Personal attributes (primary, verified)** — [Meta Personal Attributes](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes/)
- Ads may not "assert or imply personal attributes", which include physical or mental health and medical conditions. They may not "Imply knowledge of Medical information of a user or user's family". Allowed example: "Bulimia counseling available". Disallowed example: **"Do you have diabetes?"** — [Meta Personal Attributes](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes/)
- Practitioner summary: Meta "largely discourages second-person language in ad copy" around health. Phrasing like "Conquer your depression" is the prohibited pattern. — [ADM](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/)

**Deception, endorsements, scams (primary, partly verified)**
- Meta's Ad Standards index now files deception under "Prohibited Commercial Practices" (formerly "Unacceptable business practices"). That page covers "deceptive pricing, unauthorized endorsements" and points to the matching Community Standard. — [Meta Prohibited Commercial Practices](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/deceptive-content/prohibited-commercial-practices/); [Ad Standards index](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/)
- The Fraud/Scams Community Standard prohibits "Celebrity-bait scams, which involve falsely using the name, likeness, or imagery of a public figure, together with misleading tactics to bait people into engaging with the content in an attempt to scam or defraud." — [Meta Fraud & Scams CS](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/community-standards/fraud-scams/)
- In March 2025 Meta brought its facial-recognition "celeb-bait" detection to the EU and UK. Public figures opt in, and ads that misuse their faces are blocked automatically, including ads made with generative AI. — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/big-tech/meta-is-bringing-new-facial-recognition-tools-to-the-uk-eu-and-south-korea-185303065.html); [Channels TV/AFP, 5 Mar 2025](https://www.channelstv.com/2025/03/05/meta-targets-fake-celebrity-ads-in-europe-s-korea/)

**Health-data / lower-funnel restrictions (secondary; Meta's own Help Center text not retrieved)**
- From roughly January 2025, Meta classifies data sources (domains/apps) by category. Health & Wellness domains get "Core setup" restrictions, which strip URL paths/parameters and custom parameters. Some also get partial or full restriction of standard events. Polar Analytics says this applies "across the US, EU, and UK" and blocks "Purchase", "Add to Cart" and similar events. — [Polar Analytics](https://www.polaranalytics.com/post/2025-metas-tracking-restrictions-for-health-wellness-are-here----heres-how-to-fix-it)
- Three levels: Level 1 (core setup: custom URL and event parameters), Level 2 (standard events "Purchase, Lead, InitiateCheckout, AddToCart, Schedule, CompleteRegistration"), Level 3 (all events, including PageView). Restriction applies "at the data source level… regardless of your ad creative". Classification triggers include "Supplement names, weight loss claims, condition-specific product descriptions". Meta "sometimes applies restrictions to specific regions before applying them globally", sometimes starting with EU traffic. Appeals take 3–7 days, can be resubmitted every 30 days, and are "frequently denied" for genuinely restricted categories. — [Zappush](https://www.zappush.com/blog/meta-data-sharing-restrictions-applied-heres-how-to-fix-it)
- Practitioners disagree on workarounds. Some search-surfaced guides suggest renaming Purchase to a custom event (e.g., "OrderCompleted") — [Polar Analytics](https://www.polaranalytics.com/post/2025-metas-tracking-restrictions-for-health-wellness-are-here----heres-how-to-fix-it), [Aimerce](https://newsletter.aimerce.ai/p/meta-pixel-changes-for-health-wellness-brands-a-quick-update). Zappush says renaming events, switching to CAPI and subdomain tricks do not work. — [Zappush](https://www.zappush.com/blog/meta-data-sharing-restrictions-applied-heres-how-to-fix-it); also Stape: "Conversions API does not bypass the data sharing rules" — [Stape](https://stape.io/news/meta-data-sharing-restrictions-healthcare)
- Meta has also stepped up enforcement against custom audiences/conversions with health-related names (e.g., "arthritis_interest_list"). Recommended mitigations: neutral naming, sanitised URL paths and parameters, form submissions that send status only. — [ADM](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/)
- Advertisers can have "their ads limited or disapproved, or even have their accounts locked" for policy violations. — [ADM](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/)
- "Landing-page parity": the destination must back up every claim in the ad and must not add new claims. — [ADM](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/)

### Inferences
- **The "wearables" clause is a direct risk for patches.** A transdermal patch can reasonably be read as a "wearable product", so copy implying the patch alone causes weight loss ("just stick it on and the kilos melt away") fits the prohibited pattern exactly. Safer framing: present the patch as one part of a routine that includes diet and movement, never as the sole cause of results. (Interpretation; Meta does not define "wearable" in the text I retrieved.)
- **Several existing angles collide with Meta text, not just with law:** "insulin/blood sugar" (Diabetes is on the cure-claim list), "thyroid" (on the list), and "metformin/Ozempic" (implies treating diabetes or obesity). Wording like "Have you got PCOS / menopause belly / high cortisol?" breaches the personal-attributes rule, which bans "Do you have diabetes?".
- **Treat before/after as off-limits for a weight-loss product.** The primary text only names cosmetic before/after. The secondary sources conflict, and the "pinching fat" ban remains.
- **The advertorial is reviewed too.** Under landing-page parity, a compliant ad pointing to an advertorial with cure claims, fake comments or a fake news masthead can still be rejected. Repeated rejections are the usual route to account or Business Manager restriction. (Practitioner reading of ADM plus the policy structure.)
- **Data restrictions:** a domain that names berberine, weight loss or blood sugar is likely classified Health & Wellness. Optimising for Purchase may be blocked, especially for EU traffic. Two options are commonly used: optimise for landing-page views/engagement, or keep a cleaner domain. Deliberately disguising health events to get around Meta's restriction could breach Meta's Business Tools terms. It could also raise GDPR Art. 9 (special-category health data) questions, because buying a "blood sugar" product can reveal health status. This is my inference, not something I verified in Meta's terms.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve Meta's Business Help Center article on health & wellness data-sharing restrictions, so the exact EU scope and dates come from practitioner sources only (Polar says US/EU/UK; Zappush describes region-by-region rollout).
- I could not retrieve the full text of Meta's "low-quality or disruptive landing page" / "sensational content" policies in this session. I found no primary Meta text that names "fake news-site formats" or "fake comments". These appear to be enforced through the Prohibited Commercial Practices and Fraud/Scams standards and through landing-page review, which is inference.
- I found no first-party data on ban rates for weight-loss advertisers.

## 2. EU law: claims regulation, berberine status, UCPD blacklist, DSA/AI Act, and how a transdermal patch is legally classified

### Takeaway
A **transdermal patch is not a food**: EU law defines food as something "ingested". So the patch cannot lawfully be a food supplement, and the EU health-claims register cannot "authorise" its claims. It falls under either the **Cosmetics Regulation** (only cosmetic purposes allowed: appearance, skin, care) or **medicinal-product law**. Presenting it as treating or preventing disease, or as changing blood sugar, metabolism or fat through systemic action, makes it a medicinal product "by presentation" and/or "by function". In any case berberine has **no authorised EU health claim**. EFSA's **January 2026 draft opinion** found **no safe intake level** for berberine; the final opinion is expected in early 2027 and could lead to an EU-wide restriction or ban in foods. The UCPD blacklist covers undisclosed advertorials (pt 11), false cure claims (pt 17), false urgency (pt 7), fake consumer personas (pt 22) and fake reviews (pts 23b/23c), regardless of product category.

### Cited Findings
**Classification of the product**
- "'food'… means any substance or product… intended to be, or reasonably expected to be ingested by humans" (Reg. 178/2002 Art. 2). — [Reg 178/2002 Art 2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2002/178/article/2)
- The Bulgarian Ordinance on food supplements (Art. 3(2)) says food supplements "се представят и предлагат на пазара като храни" (are presented and marketed as foods). — [Наредба за хранителните добавки (consolidated, DV 62/7 Jul 2026)](https://www.mzh.government.bg/media/filer_public/2026/07/08/naredba_za_khranit_elnite_dobavki.pdf)
- Cosmetic product = "any substance or mixture intended to be placed in contact with the external parts of the human body… with a view exclusively or mainly to cleaning them, perfuming them, changing their appearance, protecting them, keeping them in good condition or correcting body odours" (Reg. 1223/2009 Art. 2(1)(a)). — [Reg 1223/2009 Art 2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2009/1223/article/2)
- Medicinal product = "(a) Any substance or combination of substances presented as having properties for treating or preventing disease in human beings; or (b) … administered … with a view to restoring, correcting or modifying physiological functions by exerting a pharmacological, immunological or metabolic action" (Dir. 2001/83 Art. 1(2)). — [Dir 2001/83 Art 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2001/83/article/1)
- From the EU Cosmetics Borderline Manual: "The patch as such is an article and therefore not a cosmetic product. However, the substance or preparation released by the patch may be a cosmetic product… Alternatively… a medicinal product 'by presentation' or 'by function'." For patches, "consideration has to be given inter alia as to whether active ingredients enter the general blood circulation thereby modifying physiological functions… One criterion… whether the patch is occlusive… Occlusive patches may allow for a deeper penetration… making the substance systemically available." The manual also says cellulite-reducing products "may be a medicinal product by virtue of function". (Manual v2.2, Feb 2016, non-binding; national authorities decide case by case.) — [Cosmetics Borderline Manual v2.2](https://ec.europa.eu/docsroom/documents/23104/attachments/1/translations/en/renditions/native)
- Secondary summary: classification depends on whether the primary purpose is cosmetic or therapeutic; national competent authorities decide in cases of doubt. — [ChemLinked](https://cosmetic.chemlinked.com/expert-article/borderline-products-in-the-eu-cosmetic-products-or-not); [Certified Cosmetics](https://www.certifiedcosmetics.com/blog/cosmetic-borderline-products/are-skin-patches-cosmetics-or-medical-devices/)
- The Cosmetic Claims Regulation 655/2013 sets six common criteria: legal compliance, truthfulness, evidential support, honesty, fairness and informed decision-making. Key lines: "Ingredient claims referring to the properties of a specific ingredient shall not imply that the finished product has the same properties when it does not"; "Claims… shall be supported by adequate and verifiable evidence"; "Presentations of a product's performance shall not go beyond the available supporting evidence". — [Reg 655/2013 Annex](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2013/655/annex)

**Claims regulation for foods (relevant if anyone argues the product is a supplement, and as a benchmark regulators use)**
- Reg. 1924/2006 Art. 12 forbids health claims that "(a) … suggest that health could be affected by not consuming the food; (b) … make reference to the rate or amount of weight loss; (c) … make reference to recommendations of individual doctors or health professionals…". The EU text of (c) continues "and other associations not referred to in Article 11"; the linked UK-retained version has amended wording after "professionals". — [Reg 1924/2006 Art 12 (legislation.gov.uk)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2006/1924/article/12); [EUR-Lex 32006R1924](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32006R1924)
- In Bulgaria, labels, presentation and advertising of food supplements must not attribute properties of "предпазват, лекуват или излекуват болести при човека" (preventing, treating or curing human disease) or refer to such properties. They also must not suggest that a balanced diet cannot provide adequate nutrients (Ordinance Art. 15(1)-(2)). — [Наредба за хранителните добавки](https://www.mzh.government.bg/media/filer_public/2026/07/08/naredba_za_khranit_elnite_dobavki.pdf)

**Berberine: claims and safety status**
- "No health claims are authorised at European level for berberine-containing plants, despite their common marketing for blood glucose and cholesterol regulation" (ANSES, 25 Nov 2019). ANSES found pharmacological effects from 400 mg/day. It advised that children, pregnant and breastfeeding women, **diabetics** and people with liver or heart disorders avoid berberine, and warned of drug interactions. — [ANSES](https://www.anses.fr/en/content/use-berberine-containing-plants-food-supplements)
- The European Commission put all botanical health claims "on hold", with over 2,000 botanical claims affected. — [ScienceDirect review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1756464622000068)
- EFSA NDA Panel adopted a **draft opinion on 29 Jan 2026** under **Art. 8(2) of Reg. 1925/2006**. It was "unable to establish a safe level of intake", noted in-vitro genotoxicity signals, carcinogenic activity in rodents and idiosyncratic liver injury in humans, and listed data gaps. The **public consultation was extended to 10 Jul 2026**. — [Supplemental.pl](https://supplemental.pl/en/knowledge/berberine-efsa-draft-opinion-2026-art8-regulation-1925-2006-2/); [NutraIngredients, 10 Mar 2026](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2026/03/10/no-safe-intake-level-for-berberine-efsa-opens-consultation/); [EFSA call for data](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/call/call-data-scientific-opinion-evaluation-safety-use-plant-preparations-containing-berberine)
- Possible outcomes are listing in Annex III of Reg. 1925/2006: Part A (prohibition), Part B (restrictions such as dose limits and warnings) or Part C (Union scrutiny, with 4 years to submit safety data). The Commission decides through comitology after EFSA's final opinion. Belgium limits berberine to 10 mg/day in supplements, while market products typically contain 500–1,500 mg/day. — [Supplemental.pl](https://supplemental.pl/en/knowledge/berberine-efsa-draft-opinion-2026-art8-regulation-1925-2006-2/)
- The final EFSA opinion is expected in **early 2027**. One regulatory lawyer said "a full ban on berberine-containing plant preparations in food supplements, cannot be ruled out." — [NutraIngredients, 31 Jul 2026](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2026/07/31/safety-in-question-what-is-the-future-of-berberine/)

**UCPD 2005/29/EC: Annex I blacklist (banned in all circumstances; verified text)** — [UCPD Annex I](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2005/29/annex/I)
- Pt 2: "Displaying a trust mark, quality mark or equivalent without having obtained the necessary authorisation."
- Pt 4: "Claiming that a trader… or a product has been approved, endorsed or authorised by a public or private body when he/it has not…"
- Pt 7: "Falsely stating that a product will only be available for a very limited time, or that it will only be available on particular terms for a very limited time, in order to elicit an immediate decision…"
- Pt 11: "Using editorial content in the media to promote a product where a trader has paid for the promotion without making that clear in the content or by images or sounds clearly identifiable by the consumer (advertorial)."
- Pt 13: "Promoting a product similar to a product made by a particular manufacturer in such a manner as deliberately to mislead the consumer into believing that the product is made by that same manufacturer when it is not."
- Pt 17: "Falsely claiming that a product is able to cure illnesses, dysfunction or malformations."
- Pt 20: describing a product as "free" when the consumer must pay anything beyond unavoidable delivery costs.
- Pt 22: "Falsely claiming or creating the impression that the trader is not acting for purposes relating to his trade… or falsely representing oneself as a consumer."
- Art. 7(2): it is a misleading omission to "fail[s] to identify the commercial intent of the commercial practice if not already apparent from the context". — [UCPD Art 7](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2005/29/article/7)
- Omnibus Directive 2019/2161 added pt 23b ("Stating that reviews of a product are submitted by consumers who have actually used or purchased the product without taking reasonable and proportionate steps to check that they originate from such consumers") and pt 23c ("Submitting or commissioning another legal or natural person to submit false consumer reviews or endorsements, or misrepresenting consumer reviews or social endorsements, in order to promote products"). — [MakeInfluence summary](https://www.makeinfluence.com/en/academy/fake-and-manipulated-reviews-what-the-eu-omnibus-directive-bans); [EUR-Lex 2019/2161 PDF](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=CELEX:32019L2161&rid=2)

**AI Act (AI-generated people and "deepfakes")**
- Art. 50(4): deployers who generate or manipulate image, audio or video content constituting a deep fake "shall disclose that it has been artificially generated or manipulated". The Commission confirmed it would enforce Art. 50 from **2 Aug 2026**. Under the provisionally agreed Digital Omnibus, generative-AI systems already on the market get a grace period to 2 Dec 2026 for provider marking (watermarking) obligations. — [artificialintelligenceact.eu](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/transparency-rules-article-50/); [Hard2bit](https://hard2bit.com/en/blog/ai-act-article-50-ai-transparency-chatbots-deepfakes/)

### Inferences
- **Classification is the root issue and it limits every copy decision.** Selling the patch as a "хранителна добавка" (food supplement) is legally incoherent because it is not ingested, and it cannot be lawfully notified to БАБХ as one. The realistic positions are:
  - (a) A **cosmetic**, CPNP-notified, with a safety assessment. Allowed claims are about skin, appearance, care and "routine", supported under Reg. 655/2013. Blood-sugar, insulin, metabolism, fat-burning or appetite effects are outside cosmetic purposes.
  - (b) A **medicinal product**, which needs a marketing authorisation it almost certainly lacks. Any claim of treating, preventing or managing a disease (diabetes, insulin resistance, PCOS, hypothyroidism, high cholesterol) makes the product medicinal "by presentation", even if it is pharmacologically inert.
  - Medical device status (MDR 2017/745) does not fit. To my knowledge the MDR definition excludes products whose principal action is pharmacological or metabolic, and berberine is meant to act systemically. I did not fetch the MDR text this session.
- **"Berberine is clinically proven to lower blood sugar" is doubly risky for a patch.** The studies concern oral berberine. Reg. 655/2013 bars implying the finished product has the ingredient's properties "when it does not", and transdermal bioavailability of berberine is unestablished as far as I found. Citing oral-berberine trials to sell a patch is probably misleading under UCPD Art. 6 as well.
- **Berberine safety is now an aggravating factor.** Targeting diabetics, insulin and metformin users is exactly the group ANSES advises to avoid berberine (interaction and hypoglycaemia risk). EFSA's draft finds no safe intake. Regulators and Meta will view "for diabetics / alongside metformin" messaging more harshly from 2026. If the Commission lists berberine in Annex III Part A or B (expected 2027 or later), that directly binds foods. For a cosmetic, the cosmetic safety assessor would also have to address EFSA's genotoxicity concerns. That last point is my inference; I did not check cosmetic-ingredient restrictions for berberine.
- **The UCPD blacklist applies whatever the classification.** An unlabelled news-style page (pt 11 + Art. 7(2)), "cures/reverses diabetes" (pt 17), fake countdowns or "only today" offers (pt 7), "independent reviewer" personas run by the brand (pt 22), fabricated comment threads (pt 23c) and fake "approved by…" seals (pts 2/4) are each per-se unfair. The authority does not need to prove consumers were actually misled.
- **DSA:** the obligations fall mainly on platforms (Meta must label ads, show who paid, and keep the EU ad repository/Ad Library). Practical consequence for advertisers: every EU ad is publicly archived and searchable, which makes it easy for regulators, journalists and competitors to document violations. This is from general knowledge and was not re-verified this session.

### Gaps
- I did not re-verify the current version of the Cosmetics Borderline Manual. The cited v2.2 (2016) has later versions, and the patch wording may have changed.
- I did not verify whether berberine or Berberis extracts are restricted in the Cosmetics Regulation annexes or CosIng.
- I did not retrieve the MDR Art. 2(1) text or the Commission's MD borderline manual ([link, not reviewed](https://health.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2023-09/md_borderline_manual_en.pdf)), so whether it has a specific "slimming patch" entry is unverified.
- I did not verify current transitional rules (Reg. 1924/2006 Art. 28) on using "on hold" botanical claims under national rules, or whether any Berberis claim is in the on-hold list. Not decisive for a non-food patch anyway.
- I found no EU-level CPC network action specifically against weight-loss advertorials. I also did not verify the status of the Commission's planned "Digital Fairness Act" (dark patterns, influencer marketing) as of Oct 2026.

## 3. Bulgaria: БАБХ, КЗП, ИАЛ, КЗК, НСС and enforcement against weight-loss and fake-news advertorials

### Takeaway
Bulgaria applies the EU framework through: the Food Act and supplement ordinance (БАБХ, supplements only); the Consumer Protection Act (КЗП, unfair practices, including against fake news-site weight-loss pages); the Competition Protection Act (КЗК, misleading and comparative advertising, with fines that have reached 2.5% of turnover); and the medicines act ЗЛПХМ (ИАЛ). ЗЛПХМ bans internet advertising of prescription medicines, bans medical specialists **and people "presenting themselves" as medical specialists** from advertising medicines, and fines advertising of unapproved medicines at 10,000–20,000 BGN (up to 3× for companies). In 2026 enforcement escalated sharply: in **May 2026** ГДБОП and prosecutors broke up a **€240–250m supplement scam** that used AI-faked doctors and journalists, and Bulgarian celebrities (e.g., Kamen Donev) have publicly disowned deepfake ads.

### Cited Findings
**Food law (supplements, БАБХ)**
- Food supplements must be registered and entered in the register under **Arts. 79–80 of the Food Act (Закон за храните)**. Distance selling requires registration under Art. 61. Supplements can be sold only via registered food outlets, pharmacies and drugstores (Ordinance Arts. 4, 6). — [Наредба за хранителните добавки](https://www.mzh.government.bg/media/filer_public/2026/07/08/naredba_za_khranit_elnite_dobavki.pdf)
- Mandatory label and advertising information includes the recommended daily dose, a warning not to exceed it, a warning that the product does not replace a varied diet, and the **register number and date** (Art. 14(2)). Labels and ads must not attribute disease prevention, treatment or cure properties (Art. 15). БАБХ may temporarily ban or restrict a supplement that presents a health risk (Art. 5(2)). The ordinance was amended in DV 35/25 Apr 2025 and DV 62/7 Jul 2026. — [Наредба](https://www.mzh.government.bg/media/filer_public/2026/07/08/naredba_za_khranit_elnite_dobavki.pdf)

**Medicines law (ИАЛ / Ministry of Health), from the Health Minister's parliamentary answer of 12 Jun 2026** — [МЗ, отговор 4803](https://www.mh.government.bg/bg/novini/parlamentaren-kontrol/4803)
- **Art. 246 ЗЛПХМ:** "медицински специалист или лице, представящо се за медицински специалист, не може да извършва пряка или косвена реклама на лекарствени продукти" (a medical specialist, or a person presenting themselves as one, may not directly or indirectly advertise medicinal products) in print, electronic media or online.
- **Art. 248a ЗЛПХМ:** internet advertising of prescription medicines is prohibited (except approved vaccination campaigns). Art. 234(4) bans internet sales of prescription medicines.
- **Art. 290(1):** fine of 10,000–20,000 BGN for advertising unapproved medicines. **Art. 291(1):** legal entities and sole traders face up to 3× the maximum. Art. 287b: 5,000–10,000 BGN for illegal online pharmaceutical trade.
- ИАЛ monitors websites, publishes warnings about illegal GLP-1 receptor agonists and refers cases to ГДБОП. It cannot itself shut down websites.
- Advertising of medicinal products is also governed by **Наредба № 1 от 25.01.2012** (issued under Art. 249 ЗЛПХМ). — [ИАЛ/BDA PDF](https://www.bda.bg/images/stories/documents/regulations/naredbi/NAREDBA_za_iziskvaniqta_kym_reklamata_na_lp.pdf)

**Consumer protection (КЗП)**
- КЗП found an **unfair commercial practice** in how the weight-loss supplement **Caralluma** was presented on **www.news2today.net/bgm/**, a news-style domain. - **Who would come after FitPatches, and how:**

  - КЗК: misleading or comparative advertising. Competitors can file complaints, and fines are turnover-based.
  - ИАЛ / МЗ: if copy presents the patch as treating diabetes or obesity or as an alternative to Ozempic or metformin (medicinal by presentation), or uses doctor personas.
  - РЗИ / Ministry of Health for cosmetics market surveillance (from general knowledge, not verified this session).
  - БАБХ: only if the product is (wrongly) marketed as a "хранителна добавка".
  - ГДБОП / prosecutors: criminal exposure arises when the pattern looks like fraud (fake celebrities or doctors, fake media brands, untraceable trader). After May 2026 the authorities and the public strongly associate "fake news page + doctor + COD supplement" with organised fraud.
- **Art. 246 ЗЛПХМ ("лице, представящо се за медицински специалист") is the key Bulgarian rule against fictional "Д-р …" characters,** if the product is presented with medicinal properties. Combined with UCPD pt 4/22/23c, a fictional doctor endorsing a "blood-sugar" patch is high-risk on several grounds at once.

## 4. Using drug names (Ozempic, Mounjaro, metformin) in ads and advertorials

### Takeaway
Naming prescription drugs in a patch advertorial carries several risks at once:
- **Meta**: it implies treating diabetes or obesity, and it trips health-data classification.
- **Medicinal-by-presentation and ЗЛПХМ**: positioning the patch as an alternative to a prescription drug.
- **UCPD**: pt 13 if it suggests a link to the drug maker, pt 17 if it implies a cure.
- **Comparative-advertising and trademark** exposure.
US case law shows trademark and false-advertising litigation around "Ozempic" comparisons. I found no EU case against a supplement or patch seller specifically. Risk level: **high on Meta, medium-high legally**. Severity rises with how directly the copy claims equivalence ("natural Ozempic", "replaces metformin").

### Cited Findings
- Novo Nordisk sued LifeRxMd on 6 Sep 2024 for infringing the Ozempic and Wegovy trademarks and for false advertising by presenting products as generic versions. It filed 13 suits in 2023 and 3 more in July 2024 against compounders, clinics and med spas (US). — [Clyde & Co](https://www.clydeco.com/en/insights/2024/10/the-latest-weight-loss-craze-litigation); [Syracuse Law Review](https://lawreview.syr.edu/novo-nordisk-files-suit-centered-around-their-ozempic-and-wegovy-medications-again/amp)
- A proposed US class action targets "Metabolism Ignite", marketed as **"Nature's Ozempic"**, alleging it cannot match the drug it is compared with. — [Law360](https://www.law360.com/articles/2487949/user-says-nature-s-ozempic-can-t-keep-weigh-loss-promise)
- In Bulgaria, internet advertising of prescription medicines is prohibited (Art. 248a ЗЛПХМ). ИАЛ actively publishes warnings on illegal GLP-1 products online. — [МЗ, отговор 4803](https://www.mh.government.bg/bg/novini/parlamentaren-kontrol/4803)
- A product "presented as having properties for treating or preventing disease" is a medicinal product (Dir. 2001/83 Art. 1(2)(a)). — [Dir 2001/83 Art 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2001/83/article/1)
- UCPD Annex I pt 13 (misleading consumers into thinking a product is made by a particular manufacturer) and pt 17 (false cure claims). — [UCPD Annex I](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2005/29/annex/I)
- Meta's personal-attributes rule bans "Do you have diabetes?" and its H&W policy bans cure claims for Diabetes. — [Meta Personal Attributes](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes/); [Meta H&W](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness/)

### Inferences
- **Graded risk (practitioner judgement, not law):**
  - *Highest:* "Натурален Оземпик" (natural Ozempic), "като Оземпик, но без игли" (like Ozempic but without needles), "спрях метформина" (I stopped metformin), "по-добро от инсулин" (better than insulin), and before/after with drug comparisons. These imply equivalence or substitution of a prescription therapy: medicinal by presentation, UCPD pt 17, and a safety concern if people stop medication.
  - *High:* "Алтернатива на GLP-1 инжекциите" (an alternative to GLP-1 injections), or a headline built on the Ozempic trend that implies a similar mechanism.
  - *Medium:* a neutral news-context mention in the advertorial body ("В ерата на Оземпик много хора търсят…", "In the Ozempic era many people look for…") with explicit "не е лекарство, не замества лечение" (not a medicine, does not replace treatment). Still likely to trigger Meta health classification.
  - *Lower:* no drug names in ads at all; on the advertorial, generic lifestyle framing.
- **Comparative-advertising law** (Directive 2006/114/EC, transposed in ЗЗК Arts. 34 ff.; not fetched this session) generally requires comparing products that meet the same needs, and forbids taking unfair advantage of a competitor's trademark reputation or presenting goods as imitations of a trademarked product. An unauthorised cosmetic patch and a prescription GLP-1 agonist arguably do not "meet the same needs" lawfully, so a comparison is hard to justify.
- **Never tell readers to stop or reduce metformin, insulin or other medication**, even in a "testimonial". Given ANSES's warnings on berberine with diabetes drugs and the May 2026 Bulgarian scam narrative, this is the single most dangerous message for both legal and reputational liability.

### Gaps
- I found no EU or Bulgarian court or regulator decision on a supplement or patch advertiser using "Ozempic" in ads, and no evidence of Novo Nordisk trademark actions in Bulgaria. This is not evidence that none exist.
- I did not verify Novo Nordisk's or Lilly's EU trademark-enforcement policy on "Ozempic"/"Mounjaro" in third-party advertising.

## 5. Testimonials, results, doctor characters, stock photos and AI-generated people

### Takeaway
Testimonials are lawful only if they come from **real, verifiable customers** (the trader must take "reasonable and proportionate steps" to check them), are **not edited into claims the brand itself could not make**, and are **not invented or commissioned** (UCPD pts 23b/23c). Fictional "Д-р" personas, fake experts or fake media quotes are per-se unfair (pts 4/22/23c). In Bulgaria they also run into Art. 246 ЗЛПХМ when the product is presented medicinally. From **2 Aug 2026** AI-generated realistic people or voices may need an AI-generated disclosure under AI Act Art. 50(4). Presenting an AI face as a real customer is misleading in any case.

### Cited Findings
- UCPD pts 23b/23c (reviews must be verified; no false or commissioned reviews or "social endorsements"). — [MakeInfluence](https://www.makeinfluence.com/en/academy/fake-and-manipulated-reviews-what-the-eu-omnibus-directive-bans); [EUR-Lex 2019/2161](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=CELEX:32019L2161&rid=2)
- UCPD pt 22 (trader posing as consumer) and pt 4 (false claim of approval or endorsement). — [UCPD Annex I](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2005/29/annex/I)
- For foods, health claims referring to "recommendations of individual doctors or health professionals" are banned (Reg. 1924/2006 Art. 12(c)). The Bulgarian medicines act bars medical specialists and anyone "представящо се за медицински специалист" from advertising medicines (Art. 246 ЗЛПХМ). — [Reg 1924/2006 Art 12](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2006/1924/article/12); [МЗ, отговор 4803](https://www.mh.government.bg/bg/novini/parlamentaren-kontrol/4803)
- Reg. 655/2013 (cosmetics): "Marketing communications shall not imply that expressions of opinions are verified claims unless the opinion reflects verifiable evidence"; performance presentations "shall not go beyond the available supporting evidence". — [Reg 655/2013 Annex](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2013/655/annex)
- Meta's H&W policy lets supplement ads "illustrate people using the product… and clearly indicate the time taken to achieve noticeable results", while banning "promises of specific outcomes within a set timeframe without disclaimers or qualifiers". — [Meta H&W](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness/)
- AI Act Art. 50(4) deepfake disclosure applies from 2 Aug 2026, with Commission enforcement confirmed. — [artificialintelligenceact.eu](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/transparency-rules-article-50/); [Hard2bit](https://hard2bit.com/en/blog/ai-act-article-50-ai-transparency-chatbots-deepfakes/)
- In March 2025 Meta deployed facial recognition against ads misusing public figures' faces in the EU, including AI-generated ones. — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/big-tech/meta-is-bringing-new-facial-recognition-tools-to-the-uk-eu-and-south-korea-185303065.html)
- The KZP Caralluma case objected to result claims without evidence and to an illusory money-back guarantee. — [ECC.bg](https://www.ecc.bg/bg/blog/article/ustanovena-neloyalna-targovska-praktika-pri-predstavyane-na-hranitelna-dobavka-za-otslabvane)

### Inferences (practical checklist)
- **Real customers only.** Keep written consent (GDPR plus image rights) and proof of purchase for each story. Record who wrote the text. Disclose any incentive such as a free product or discount ("получи продукта безплатно" (received the product free)).
- **No numbers on weight loss in the advertorial.** "-12 кг за 30 дни" (-12 kg in 30 days) is a rate/amount-of-weight-loss claim. It is banned outright for foods (Art. 12(b)), unsubstantiated for a cosmetic (655/2013), and "clickbait… specific outcomes within a set timeframe" on Meta. If any result appears, it must be the customer's own experience, non-quantified or accompanied by "Резултатите са индивидуални и зависят от храненето и движението" (results are individual and depend on diet and exercise), and **typical**. A "results not typical" disclaimer does not cure an atypical headline under the UCPD "overall impression" test (my interpretation).
- **Doctors and experts:** use a real, named professional who has actually reviewed the content, disclose any payment, and keep them away from disease or medicinal statements (Art. 246 ЗЛПХМ risk for both parties). Do not invent "Д-р Иванова, ендокринолог" (Dr Ivanova, endocrinologist). Do not use real doctors' names or photos without consent; post-May 2026 this pattern is associated with fraud.
- **Stock photos:** fine for illustration only if clearly not presented as the reviewer ("Снимката е илюстративна" (illustrative photo)). Never use a stock face as "Мария, 47, Пловдив" (Maria, 47, Plovdiv) with a result attached.
- **AI-generated people:** if realistic and depicting a "customer", "doctor" or "journalist", it is misleading under the UCPD whether or not it is disclosed. If AI imagery is used at all (e.g., illustrative), label it "Изображението е генерирано с AI" (image generated with AI). From 2 Aug 2026 this is plausibly required under Art. 50(4); whether a synthetic person who resembles no real individual is a "deep fake" is debated.
- **Comments sections:** no fabricated Facebook-style comment widgets. Show only real, moderated reviews collected through a verified-purchase mechanism (pt 23b), and do not suppress negative reviews (pt 23c "misrepresenting consumer reviews").

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the European Commission's UCPD Guidance (2021) passages on endorsements, influencers and "typical results", or the КЗП/НСС influencer recommendations.
- I found no Bulgarian authority guidance on typical-results disclaimers. The point above is practitioner interpretation.
- US FTC contrast (Endorsement Guides 2023, Consumer Reviews Rule 2024) was not researched beyond the "Nature's Ozempic" US litigation.

## 6. Practical compliant-yet-persuasive copy patterns (ad + advertorial)

### Takeaway
The lawful persuasive space for a berberine **patch** in Bulgaria is narrow. The legally clean levers are: transparent "РЕКЛАМА" (advertisement) labelling; a brand-owned editorial format rather than a fake news masthead; habit, routine and convenience storytelling; factual ingredient information that does not transfer oral-berberine study results to the patch; verified, non-quantified customer experiences; real offer mechanics (genuine guarantees, honest stock and deadlines, COD transparency); and avoiding "you have [condition]" language. Most existing angles (metformin, Ozempic, insulin, blood sugar, cholesterol, thyroid, PCOS, cortisol) are disease or physiological-function framings. They are high-risk on Meta and arguably make the product medicinal by presentation. Menopause, visceral fat and "honest review" can be reworked.

### Cited Findings
- Advertorials must be clearly identifiable as paid (UCPD pt 11; Art. 7(2) commercial intent). — [UCPD Annex I](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2005/29/annex/I); [UCPD Art 7](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2005/29/article/7)
- КЗП targeted a news-style domain (news2today.net) and claims such as "интензивно стопяват корема" and "ускоряват обмяната на веществата". — [ECC.bg](https://www.ecc.bg/bg/blog/article/ustanovena-neloyalna-targovska-praktika-pri-predstavyane-na-hranitelna-dobavka-za-otslabvane)
- Meta prohibits negative self-perception statements, "pinching fat", sole-wearable result claims, sensational timeframe promises and Diabetes/Thyroid cure claims. Personal-attribute questions such as "Do you have diabetes?" are banned. — [Meta H&W](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness/); [Meta Personal Attributes](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes/)
- Cosmetic claims must meet the six common criteria; ingredient claims must not imply that the finished product has the ingredient's properties when it does not. — [Reg 655/2013 Annex](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2013/655/annex)
- No authorised EU health claims exist for berberine-containing plants. — [ANSES](https://www.anses.fr/en/content/use-berberine-containing-plants-food-supplements)
- Landing-page parity: the destination must not add claims the ad does not make. — [ADM](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/)

### Inferences: angle-by-angle risk map and safer rewrites (practitioner interpretation; Bulgarian copy is illustrative)

| Existing angle | Main problems | Risk | Safer direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Metformin" / "Ozempic" / "insulin" | Drug comparison makes it medicinal by presentation; UCPD pt 17; Meta Diabetes cure list and personal attributes; ЗЛПХМ; trademark; berberine-diabetes safety | **Very high** | Drop drug names from ads. In the advertorial, at most a cultural hook ("Всички говорят за инжекциите за отслабване. Ние решихме да поговорим за навиците." (Everyone talks about weight-loss injections. We decided to talk about habits.)) plus "FitPatches не е лекарство и не замества предписано лечение. Ако приемате лекарства, консултирайте се с лекар." (FitPatches is not a medicine and does not replace prescribed treatment. If you take medication, consult a doctor.) |
| "Blood sugar" / "cholesterol" | Physiological-function or disease claim; no authorised claim; oral-study transfer misleading | **High** | Talk about routine and energy without mechanisms: "Част от сутрешния ми ритуал, заедно с по-добрата закуска" (part of my morning ritual, together with a better breakfast). No glucose meters, no numbers. |
| "Thyroid" / "PCOS" | Diseases; Thyroid is on Meta's list; personal attributes | **Very high** | Drop. |
| "Menopause" | Not a disease, but "your menopause belly" is a personal attribute and negative self-perception | Medium | Third-person life-stage story: "Жените над 45 често казват, че старите навици вече не работят…" (Women over 45 often say their old habits no longer work…). No "you", no body-shaming, no fat-pinch imagery. |
| "Cortisol" / "visceral fat" | Physiological or medical mechanism claims; "melts belly fat" pattern as in the КЗП Caralluma case | High | Shift to lifestyle drivers (sleep, stress, walking) as context. The patch is a reminder or part of a routine, never the mechanism. |
| "Honest review" | Brand-written "independent" review is UCPD pt 22 + pt 11 + Art. 7(2) | High if undisclosed | Label it "Платена публикация / Реклама на FitPatches" (paid publication / FitPatches advertisement) at the top, use a real customer's own words with consent, disclose incentives, include negatives and typical experience. |

General patterns:
- **Label and format:** put "РЕКЛАМА" or "Платена публикация" above the headline, plus the company name, ЕИК (company ID) and address in the footer (the Caralluma case faulted missing trader identity). Use a brand "journal" design. Do not imitate bTV, Nova, 24 Chasa or other real outlets' logos or typography (pt 11 + trademark + pt 4), and do not use fake "As seen on" logos.
- **Headlines:** use curiosity and identity framing without condition attribution or numeric promises. Examples: "Как 3 жени от Варна промениха вечерните си навици" (How 3 women from Varna changed their evening habits), "Защо все повече хора избират пластири вместо капсули" (Why more and more people choose patches over capsules). Format and convenience claims are truthful and provable.
- **Ingredient section:** factual and sourced, e.g., "Берберинът е алкалоид, който се съдържа в растения като берберис" (Berberine is an alkaloid found in plants such as barberry). Do not write "клинично доказано понижава кръвната захар" (clinically proven to lower blood sugar), and do not attach oral-supplement trials to the patch.
- **Results:** no before/after for weight loss, no kg-per-week figures, and no "без диета и спорт" (no diet or exercise needed); the last also hits Meta's "solely by wearable" clause. Show product-in-use imagery: application, daily routine, real customers with consent.
- **Urgency and offers:** only real deadlines and real stock. No timers that reset on reload, and no "само днес" (today only) when the offer runs permanently (pt 7). Do not call items "безплатно" (free) if shipping or the bundle price hides the cost (pt 20). A money-back guarantee is a strong honest converter if it is actually honoured with a reachable trader identity.
- **Safety copy as a trust lever:** include "Не е подходящ за бременни, кърмачки, деца, хора с диабет на лечение или чернодробни заболявания; консултирайте се с лекар" (not suitable for pregnant or breastfeeding women, children, people with diabetes on treatment or liver disease; consult a doctor). This mirrors ANSES's risk groups and reduces liability, at some cost to conversion with the diabetic segment. That segment is the highest-risk audience anyway.
- **Ad-to-page consistency:** Meta reviews both, and every advertorial claim must be one the brand can substantiate.
- **Data setup:** consider a neutral domain or brand and neutral URL paths and event names. Expect Purchase optimisation limits for health-classified domains, and do not rely on renaming events as a durable fix (Zappush vs. Polar disagreement above).

### Gaps
- I found no primary evidence of how large compliant EU brands phrase "GLP-1-adjacent" claims for supplements or patches, and no compliant-brand copy examples with sources.
- Conversion impact of compliant vs. non-compliant advertorials is not quantified in any source I found.
- Whether FitPatches' patch is currently CPNP-notified as a cosmetic, registered with БАБХ, or neither is unknown. This determines which regulator and claim regime applies first.
